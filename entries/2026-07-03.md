Dear diary,

Man, what a week. Or rather, what a *year*. It’s finally Friday, July 3rd, and I can almost taste the barbecue for tomorrow. Professor Davies just sent out the grades for CS 2, and somehow I pulled off an A-. Not bad, considering how much time I spent trying to debug that multi-threaded web server simulation. My brain feels like it’s been scraped clean with a scouring pad.

But even with the relief of summer break truly starting, my mind keeps circling back to this conversation I had yesterday with Maya. We were in the SAC, trying to grab some late-night instant ramen, and the news was on a loop about the new Mars mission funding. Someone on the panel was talking about "humanity's inexorable march to *colonize* the red planet," and Maya just rolled her eyes.

"Seriously, Trevor," she said, stirring her noodles with an intensity usually reserved for deriving Maxwell's equations. "Do we have to use that word? 'Colonize'?"

And it hit me. Like a proton to a detector, the implications were immediate. We spend so much time here at Caltech, pushing the boundaries of what's possible, designing rockets, building rovers, simulating environments. It's all about discovery, exploration, and the advancement of science. But the language we use... it carries so much baggage.

We're taught about the grand narratives of scientific discovery, of intrepid explorers charting unknown territories. But how often do we truly confront the fact that those "unknown territories" were often already inhabited, their resources ripe for the taking, their cultures deemed "primitive"? The romanticized notion of "first contact" or "settling" new worlds on Earth was, for many, the beginning of erasure, exploitation, and subjugation. That’s what "colonialism" really boils down to, isn't it? The imposing of one's will, one's systems, one's perceived superiority onto another.

Now we're talking about Mars. No known intelligent life (yet), but what about the potential for microbial life? Or the simple fact that it's a world with its own natural processes, its own unique grandeur? Are we just going to export our terrestrial problems and ethical blind spots across the solar system? The idea of "terraforming" Mars, making it Earth-like, suddenly feels less like a miracle of engineering and more like an ultimate act of planetary arrogance, a cosmic form of colonialism if we don't consider the intrinsic value of its current state.

It makes me wonder about our responsibility as future scientists and engineers. We're the ones who will be building those spaceships, developing those technologies. Is "progress" truly progress if it doesn't come with a deep, critical understanding of history and its repeating patterns? This isn't just a history class topic; it feels like an ethical framework we need for every single design choice, every mission statement.

Maybe I'm overthinking it because my brain is still in problem-solving mode. But as I look forward to celebrating Independence Day tomorrow, a day meant to commemorate liberation from colonial rule, I can't help but feel a tension. We're celebrating freedom, but are we critically examining how our own ambitions for expansion, even into space, might echo the very systems we fought against?

It’s a lot to chew on for a freshman. I just hope that as we reach for the stars, we also look back at Earth's history with clearer eyes.

-Trevor
