Dear diary,

Sunday again, and another day that feels more like a Monday given the amount of work I've put in. The library was surprisingly full this morning, even for a summer Sunday. My SURF project, "Quantum Entanglement in Microgravity Environments," is fascinating, truly. I’m making decent progress on the experimental setup and the data analysis is… well, it’s Caltech data analysis, which is to say, dense and unforgiving.

But what's really been rattling around in my head all day is this progress report I have due on Friday. Dr. Anya Sharma, my mentor, sent me an email yesterday saying, "Trevor, remember, it's not enough to be technically proficient; you must be *communicatively literate*. Make sure your report explains the 'why' and the 'so what,' not just the 'how.'"

"Communicatively literate." I’ve been staring at that phrase, and then at a blank Word document, for about three hours straight. It's not that I don't *understand* the physics – I could write out the derivations for the entire setup by heart, probably diagram the whole thing in my sleep. But translating that into clear, compelling, *literate* prose for someone who isn't already knee-deep in quantum mechanics and fluid dynamics? That feels like trying to write a sonnet in binary.

In high school, 'literacy' mostly meant reading a novel and writing an essay about symbolism. Here, at Caltech, it's like the definition expands exponentially. There's being mathematically literate, scientifically literate, code literate (I'm still grappling with the finer points of Python’s less intuitive libraries for data visualization, so that's a work in progress). But now, "communicatively literate" for a *technical report*? It's a whole new level of challenge. My brain is wired for equations, for logical steps and proofs, not for elegant transitions between paragraphs or persuasive arguments for future funding.

I remember in my HSS (Humanities, Social Sciences, and Arts) class last semester, "The Philosophy of Science," Professor Albright kept emphasizing that even the most brilliant discoveries are useless if they can't be communicated, understood, and debated by the broader scientific community. He talked about how Newton or Einstein weren't just geniuses of thought, but also masters of articulation. I nodded along, thinking it was a nice sentiment, but my priority was acing the differential equations problem set. Now, it's hitting me just how crucial it is.

I even tried reading some scientific papers outside my immediate field today, just to see how others structure their arguments. I understood about 30% of the jargon in a biology paper, but I could appreciate the *flow* of the writing, how they built their case. It’s humbling. Makes me feel like I’m intellectually rich in one domain, but almost completely *illiterate* in another, equally vital one.

Guess I’m going to spend the rest of my Sunday trying to hack my brain into a more narrative mode. Maybe I’ll read a short story or something before bed, just to try and absorb some non-technical prose by osmosis. Being a Caltech student means always learning, always growing, even if that growth is in areas you never thought you'd have to master, like... words. Who knew?

-Trevor
