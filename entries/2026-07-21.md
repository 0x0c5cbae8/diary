Dear diary,

2026-07-21, Tuesday. Another blazing Pasadena day. You'd think after a year here, I'd be used to the heat, but no, every summer day feels like a new personal record for "most sweat produced while debugging Python."

Today was... well, it was classic Caltech, even in the summer. My SURF project, 'Project Bumblebee' (our affectionately named autonomous delivery drone, because it makes a lot of noise and is vaguely yellow), decided to throw a fit this morning. We're trying to refine its indoor navigation system, specifically how it uses visual odometry to correct for IMU drift. I thought I had everything squared away yesterday, the EKF *looked* stable in simulations. But no, the physical drone had other plans. It kept trying to hug the wall like a lost puppy looking for affection, even when there was clearly nothing there according to its depth sensors.

Hours. I spent *hours* today, hunched over my monitor, sifting through sensor logs, comparing quaternion outputs, and checking timestamps. It felt like I was back in CS 1 trying to find that one missing semicolon that broke everything, except this time the semicolon was an entire physical system with real-world noise and latency. I re-calibrated the IMU, checked the camera intrinsics for the tenth time, even peered suspiciously at the USB cable connecting the companion computer, half-expecting a tiny gremlin to jump out and confess to corrupting packets.

The frustration was immense. All the elegant theories we learn in Controls, in Computer Vision, in Embedded Systems – they felt so distant from the chaotic reality of a drone trying to navigate a hallway. It reminded me of Professor Lee's favorite saying: "In theory, there is no difference between theory and practice. In practice, there is."

Then, around 4 PM, fueled by a questionable iced coffee from the Caltech Store, it clicked. It wasn't the EKF itself, nor the sensor data. It was the *timing* of the data ingestion. A tiny, almost imperceptible delay in when the visual odometry updates were being fused with the IMU readings, causing a slight phase shift that accumulated into a noticeable error. The drone thought it was moving forward when it had actually drifted slightly to the side, and then overcorrected, leading to its wall-hugging behavior. A single `sleep(0.005)` call in the wrong place in an old library I was using.

And that, diary, is **engineering**. It's not just about drawing a circuit diagram or writing a beautiful piece of code. It's about coaxing the theoretical into the tangible. It's about the painstaking detective work of debugging a physical system, understanding all the subtle interactions between electrical, mechanical, and software components. It's about accepting that things will break, often in ways you never anticipated, and that the real learning happens when you figure out *why*. It’s messy, it's iterative, and it's incredibly satisfying when you finally find that one tiny detail that makes the whole thing work as it was designed (or, let's be honest, *better* than it was designed after all the fixes).

Tonight, Project Bumblebee is flying straight. I’m exhausted, my brain feels fried, but there's a deep sense of accomplishment. I think I'm definitely on the right path with my major choice. Time for a well-deserved, slightly-too-late dinner at Chandler.

-Trevor
